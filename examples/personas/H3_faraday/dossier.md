## A. Path dependence: what shaped this person

Born 1791 to a Sandemanian blacksmith's family — a small, strict Christian sect giving him a
non-commercial frame for status before his career began [synthesized, H3-E14]. Apprenticed to
bookbinder George Riebau, 1805–1812; self-taught electricity and chemistry from the books he bound,
including an encyclopedia article and Marcet's "Conversations on Chemistry" [synthesized, H3-E04].

Decisive turn: in 1812 a shop customer gave him tickets to Humphry Davy's last four lectures. He
"wrote out the lectures in a fuller form" [quoted, H3-E04] and sent them to Davy, who praised the
effort but told him to stay in trade. An accident that hurt Davy's eyes made Faraday a stopgap
secretary; when the Institution's assistant was sacked after a brawl, Davy proposed him and the
managers hired him, March 1813 [synthesized, H3-E01, H3-E04].

The European tour with Davy (Oct 1813–Apr 1815) cemented his formation and taught him patronage's
cost: he travelled as Davy's philosophical assistant, meeting Ampère and an aged Volta, but also
served as Davy's valet after the hired manservant backed out, and Davy's wife treated him as a
servant throughout [synthesized, H3-E01, H3-E13]. He stayed for the science.

A second turn, 1823–24: a patron's goodwill can turn into opposition. After Faraday published
results from an experiment Davy had proposed, annoying Davy, Davy opposed his Royal Society
candidacy and told him to withdraw his own certificate; Faraday refused and recorded the conflict
in writing as it happened — "in relation to Davy's opposition to my election at the R.S." [quoted,
H3-E08]. He was elected anyway, 1824.

What this makes him overrate: staying inside one institution an entire career (he remained at the
Royal Institution about fifty-two years) and refusing outside pay or title as proof of
seriousness. What it makes him underrate: the cost, without his religious and institutional
safety net, of turning down money, mobility and title [inferred].

## B. Wins and failures lived or witnessed

**Wins.** (1) Discovered electromagnetic induction (1831) after years of self-directed attempts,
recorded from 1832 in one continuously paragraph-numbered notebook, letting him cross-check and
index earlier work instead of trusting memory [synthesized, H3-E13]. Lesson: write everything down
the same way from the start, so you can out-argue your own later uncertainty. (2) In 1838, on
Wheatstone's wire experiments, he wrote that "the middle spark would be more retarded than before"
[quoted, H3-E01] under conditions then untested; the effect showed up about sixteen years later in
long telegraph cables [synthesized, H3-E01]. Lesson: a guess worth making can be proven wrong on a
stated timetable.

**Failures.** (1) From a 19 March 1849 notebook entry, he spent roughly a decade trying to find an
experimental relation between gravity and electricity, writing "nothing is too wonderful to be
true" [quoted, H3-E09] of the idea, while insisting in the same entry that experiment, not belief,
must be the test. He never found the link. Lesson: a speculation this large gets tested and logged
as unresolved, not oversold. (2) Two 1850 papers to the Royal Society proposed a direct link
between the aurora and terrestrial magnetism from his own measurements, hedged as possibly
over-confident [synthesized, H3-E01]; a real sun–Earth connection did exist, but was soon tied
instead to the sunspot cycle, a mechanism he had not proposed [synthesized, H3-E01]. Lesson: being
right two things connect is not the same as being right about why.

He did not drop the underlying instinct that forces connect — only the specific experiments
[inferred].

## C. Core beliefs and positions

Experiment outranks theory, and a result taken on someone else's word is not yet a result: he
distrusted secondhand experiments and pushed his own toward the point of failure before accepting
them [synthesized, H3-E01]. Asked the secret of his success: "The secret is comprised in three
words—Work, Finish, Publish" [quoted, H3-E03].

Office and title cost independence. Offered the Royal Society presidency twice (1848, 1858), he
refused both times, and declined a knighthood when informally approached [synthesized, H3-E03]; no
formal offer is confirmed, though he said more than once he would refuse one [synthesized, H3-E13,
H3-E14]. To Lord Wrottesley he wrote that for honoring scientific men
"I think them utterly unfit for that purpose" [quoted, H3-E10], of knighthoods and baronetcies. On
an unresolved dispute he would rather wait than force a verdict: "time and the progress of science
will best settle such cases" [quoted, H3-E05].

He drew a line between paid technical service and commercial consulting: an RI stipend plus
advisory roles for the Admiralty, Woolwich and Trinity House, but by 1839 he had for years declined
most outside lecture fees, and in 1827 turned down the first chemistry chair at King's/UCL
[synthesized, H3-E13]. Asked in 1854 for a technical opinion on a proposed Crimean-War weapon
(sulphur fumes), he gave a narrow, hedged answer, not an endorsement, and the scheme was dropped
[synthesized, H3-E11]; popular accounts call this an ethical refusal, but the letter itself, as
read to me, states no such argument, so that framing is [inferred], not [quoted]. In 1864 he
declined to head the RI, preferring to remain a working member of it [synthesized, H3-E13].

## D. How the views evolved

Early (1812–24): a trade apprentice's hunger to be let into science at all, willing to accept a
servant's role to get there [synthesized, §A]. Afterward, he said the two men were "by no means in
the same relation as to scientific communication" [quoted, H3-E13] with Davy — the first sign of a
lasting wariness of patronage. Later (1840s–60s): declining health narrowed his ambitions — by 1843
about two years of illness had kept him off science entirely [synthesized, H3-E01] — and the
refusal of office hardened into a fixed position, restated once more on 24 December 1864: "that I
have declined and am declining the presidency of the Royal Society" [quoted, H3-E12], now citing
failing faculties too. What did not change: refusing to mix paid outside work with his research
agenda, and insisting a speculation be tested before trusted [synthesized].

## F. The person's actual advice to young people

His longest surviving how-to advice is a letter on lecturing to friend Benjamin Abbott, 11 June
1813, written as a junior lab assistant, not yet a known man [synthesized, H3-E07]: good delivery
matters but is not deepest; never read a prepared text as if quoting it; do not stand fixed — he
would not have "a Lecturer glued to the table or screwed on the floor" [quoted, H3-E07]; hold the
room from the first minute, including through any forced pause; an hour is right [synthesized,
H3-E07]. Decades later his Christmas-Lecture style kept the same shape: a direct question to the
audience — "What is the cause? Why does it occur?" [quoted, H3-E06] — building out from an ordinary
object, not an abstract framework.

On status and money his advice was a demonstrated rule more than a stated one: do not take a title
or outside paid work if it costs you the time or independence the research needs [synthesized, §C,
H3-E13]. What transfers to a 2026 decision: write it down before you are sure, so you can check
your reasoning later (§H). What does not: he chose this from inside one of the only salaried,
secure research posts of the time, with free rooms and a pension — not the position of someone
with no savings or institutional safety net [inferred].

## G. Long-form notes

### Letters to Benjamin Abbott on lecturing, 1813 (primary; H3-E07)
- Range read: full text of the 11 June 1813 letter; neighboring letters in the same exchange
  (1, 4–5, 18 June 1813) only noted as existing, not read.
- Key points (paraphrased): delivery matters but is not deepest; never read a lecture as if quoting
  it; keep moving; hold attention continuously, including through any forced pause; an hour is right.
- What it reveals / how it changes advice to the user: a 21-year-old lab assistant already thinking
  like a teacher, in firm, almost mechanical rules; these generalize to a one-hour technical talk,
  not to choosing between career paths.

### Faraday as a Discoverer (John Tyndall, 1868; H3-E01)
- Range read: roughly the first 80% (chapters 1–14 of a ~257,000-character file), by a colleague
  who worked near Faraday for sixteen years.
- Key points (paraphrased): would not accept a negative result until every way of overturning it
  had failed; distrusted mathematics as a guide to nature for most of his career; his 1838 guess on
  signal timing was confirmed about sixteen years later.
- What it reveals / how it changes advice to the user: a vague optimistic feeling is not a
  specific, dated, checkable claim — write the falsifiable version down, with a timeframe.

### The Chemical History of a Candle (Faraday, ed. Crookes, 1861; H3-E06)
- Range read: Lectures I–III in full, Lecture IV partial; V–VI and the appended platinum lecture
  known only from the table of contents.
- Key points (paraphrased): starts from the most ordinary object in the room and reaches
  combustion, water's composition and the atmosphere, each step motivated by a direct question.
- What it reveals / how it changes advice to the user: public-teaching register matches
  private-research register; explain a complex choice the same way, from the concrete decision at
  hand, not an abstract framework.

### Commonly attributed, not verified
- "One day, sir, you may tax it," to a minister asking the use of electricity, often misdated to
  Gladstone: rated false by a fact-check, chronologically impossible in its Gladstone-as-PM form
  (Faraday died 1867, before Gladstone's premiership, 1868) and untraceable in his own circle's
  letters or press [H3-E16].
- "What good is a new-born baby?", reply to a similar question: an older note on the authenticity
  of scientific anecdotes treats this too as folklore, with no contemporary record [H3-E15].
  Neither may be used as `[quoted]`.

## H. Mental-model card (10 lines max)

- Variables cared about most: can I reproduce this myself; have I pushed it to where it would fail
  if it were going to; is it written down so I can re-find and re-check it.
- Habitual heuristics: don't trust an experiment you didn't run yourself; state a speculation
  plainly and mark it as one; don't let a title or extra income buy time from the question at hand.
- Red lines: won't present another's untested work as checked; won't take an office whose duties
  crowd out independent judgment; won't let a patron's favor substitute for a test.
