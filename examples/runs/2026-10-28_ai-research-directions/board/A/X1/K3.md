> Stance from: Bet on the same problem for nearly fifty years, changing institutions seven or eight times; now bets on continual learning and the experience paradigm
> Interests: Oak Lab co-founder, fundraising (2026-); bets on learning from experience, not on simulation
> Simulation based on public material; not the person's own words.

**Claim (one line):** Evaluation can only find errors and cannot create capability; what to bet on is learning algorithms that let a system know for itself whether it is wrong, and learned transition models.

**Argument:** Reinforcement learning was left out in the cold for decades; the field turned to supervised learning and lost the "reward-driven learning" line, which Barto and I then picked back up [synthesized, dossier §B failure]. Lesson: the field likes to choose judgment signals that are easy to mass-produce, not verification signals grounded in the consequences of action. Benchmarks and LLM-as-judge only tell you whether others score the system high; they do not tell the model whether it is wrong — this is exactly my verification principle [quoted, dossier §C, E14]. Oak Lab's first paper is not about a better evaluator either; it is about credit assignment in the stream of experience [synthesized, E11]. Where the analogy fails: if the evaluation is automatically verifiable (code runs, proof passes), it is no longer "human data"-style evaluation, and this part goes to L2.

**My R1 scores on related directions:** D05 5, D08 6, D09 3, D06 8.

**What fact would change my judgment:** If an LLM-as-judge could, independently of the training distribution of the system being judged, reliably identify "fabrication" without being contaminated by position bias, I would raise L1.
