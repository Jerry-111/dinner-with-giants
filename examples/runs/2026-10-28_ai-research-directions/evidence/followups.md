v6 shared evidence brief (supplement): open questions raised in the debate; facts only. Material cutoff 2026-10-28.

## Q1 Is the trustworthiness of evaluators (LLM/VLM-as-judge) getting better or worse (asked by K2/K6/M4)

- 2026, preprint "The Coin Flip Judge?" (date not verified, relayed via adaline.ai): GPT-4o-mini/GPT-4.1-mini judging the same comparison repeatedly change the outcome with an average probability of 13.6% across 29 tasks; GPT-4o-mini has a statistically significant tendency to favor the first-position answer. [Source: relayed by adaline.ai, 2026]
- 2026-04-08, the Adaline blog relays RAND team testing and JudgeBiasBench: none of the judge models it examined kept a stable ranking across benchmarks; frontier models have error rates above 50% on advanced bias tests (original paper not verified). [Source: adaline.ai, 2026-04-08]
- 2025-10, Judge's Verdict (under ICLR 2026 review): argues that a single correlation score is not enough to validate whether a judge is reliable, and proposes a new test that compares agreement patterns; this shows that meta-evaluation methodology itself is advancing. [Source: arXiv:2510.09738, 2025-10]
- From 2025-06, RewardBench 2 (UW + AI2, accepted to ICLR 2026): when the same set of models is moved to the harder new benchmark, the best models of the original leaderboard lose 20+ points on average; but some industry commentary holds that the current best judge is still slightly better than the best option available when the first version was released (no specific date given). [Source: arXiv:2506.01937; natolambert.substack.com]
- How far the public evidence goes: 2026 studies are mainly finding new patterns of instability/bias (position preference, cross-benchmark instability, self-preference); no peer-reviewed study has been seen showing an overall improvement in judge-human agreement. The meta-evaluation methods themselves (RewardBench 2, Judge's Verdict) are getting stricter. Which of the two lines is faster has no public conclusion.

## Q2 In today's AI capex, how much is real demand and how much is circular deals (asked by C2)

- 2026-08-28, Benzinga quoting Burry: cites Nvidia's 2026Q2 10-Q to question circular financing; that filing sets the cap on Nvidia's credit guarantee for OpenAI's Ohio campus (about 4.25 GW of IT load, deploying only Nvidia AI infrastructure) at $105 billion. [Source: Benzinga, 2026-08-28]
- Background: Nvidia's 2025-09 letter of intent said it planned to invest up to $100 billion to support OpenAI in building data centers; in 2026-03 Jensen Huang revised the statement, saying the actual amount was $30 billion of equity in OpenAI's $122 billion funding round (valuation $852 billion). [Source: thenextweb.com; CNBC, 2026-03]
- 2026-07-27, Bloomberg report, cited second-hand: other reports say that Nvidia is negotiating with OpenAI a lease guarantee of up to $250 billion for a 10 GW campus in Ohio; how this relates to the $105 billion cap in the 10-Q is not explained by these reports. [Source: Bloomberg, cited second-hand, 2026-07-27; not verified]
- Positive (real demand): in the 2026Q1 earnings season, Microsoft's AI business had annualized revenue of about $37 billion (+123% year on year) and a backlog of about $627 billion; Google Cloud revenue grew 63% year on year; Amazon's cloud segment grew 28%, the fastest in four years, but free cash flow over the same period fell about 95% to $1.2 billion. [Source: 2026Q1 earnings reports, via techinsights.com, 2026-05]
- How far the public evidence goes: earnings reports confirm that cloud revenue and backlog are growing fast and for real, not all circular. But Nvidia/OpenAI/Oracle have several mutual shareholding/guarantee/purchase agreements at the same time, different reports give guarantee sizes that do not match each other ($105 billion vs $250 billion), and no independent third party has calculated the share of circular transactions in total capex.

## Q3 Can JEPA / video world-model representations be used in contact and tactile settings (asked by C3/C5/M6)

- 2025-12, DreamTacVLA: uses a frozen V-JEPA 2 model to encode tactile images into latent variables and plugs them into a contact-rich manipulation policy pipeline; one of the known ways the V-JEPA series has been extended to touch. [Source: arXiv:2512.23864]
- 2026-02, Visuo-Tactile World Models: in experiments with a Franka Panda + Allegro hand + Digit 360 tactile sensors, the vision-only version's "reach and push" action often pushes empty space; adding touch keeps contact in both imagination and real execution; generalization to new tasks was not tested. [Source: arXiv:2602.06001]
- 2026-07, VT-WAM: argues that earlier visuo-tactile policies rarely truly simulate touch-driven deformation during action generation; even published tactile-enhanced methods do not solve the core problem of non-deterministic contact dynamics. [Source: arXiv:2607.02503]
- 2026, University of Washington doctoral thesis (Carolina Higuera Arias): assesses that tactile input still plays a secondary role in robot manipulation models today. [Source: digital.lib.washington.edu, 2026]
- How far the public evidence goes: in 2026 several labs attached JEPA / world-model ideas to tactile input and report positive results in their own settings, such as contact maintenance and improved prediction. But all are self-reported, with few tasks, and lack independent cross-team replication or a standardized benchmark. There is no evidence yet that a deployable success rate has been reached in industrial-grade non-deterministic contact settings (assembly, deformable objects, etc.).

## Q4 After evaluation and continual learning are commoditized, what is the next niche in the agent direction (asked by K5/M2/M3)

- 2026-07, Kirgis, Kapoor et al.: had frontier agents do "open-ended AI research" on the core problems of two unpublished NeurIPS 2026 submissions, within 6 days and a compute budget of several thousand dollars; the agents could complete the engineering implementation on their own, but made almost no substantive progress on the core research problems, and neither attempt reached publishable quality. [Source: arXiv:2607.27191]
- 2026, an ICML 2026 poster (a position paper co-signed by 24 authors) and ALMA of 2026-02: both treat "memory architectures for continual learning" as an open problem still to be solved in 2026, not an established solution already absorbed by industry; this is in tension with the question's premise (continual learning is already commoditized). [Source: icml.cc/virtual/2026/poster/67101; arXiv:2602.07755]
- 2026, ECIS 2026 conference paper: argues that research frameworks that treat the agent as a single individual hide the obstacles in multi-system collaborative environments, proposes re-modeling the agent as an autonomous system operating across multiple information systems, and points out that data/communication/security/compliance governance lacks a framework. [Source: aisel.aisnet.org/ecis2026, 2026]
- How far the public evidence goes: 2026 papers point to several candidate niches: truly open-ended agent research (still failing at present), continual-learning memory architectures, and multi-agent collaboration governance. But most are still problem statements and early method exploration; none has been shown to be "unique to academia and not yet absorbed by industry", and there is no public quantitative ranking; it remains at the level of expert judgment.

## Q5 In the cost of agent self-improvement, how much is method progress and how much is compute-driven trial and error (asked by M2/M5/K4)

- 2025-05 (ICLR 2026 poster), Darwin Gödel Machine: the cost of a two-week self-improvement run is reportedly about $22,000; SWE-bench 20.0% → 50.0%, Polyglot 14.2% → 30.7%; training a new base model is listed by the authors as heavier future work. [Source: arXiv:2505.22954]
- 2026, METR "expenditure horizon" metric (the same report as the 70-90% compute share already cited in the brief, with a different set of numbers): cost is split into three kinds: agent runs, experiment compute and a human comparison; on the NanoGPT task the best two systems gain about 1%-1.5%, with a spending ceiling of about $3,300 (model names not verified against METR's original text). [Source: metr.org, 2026]
- 2025-11, arXiv:2511.19492: infers the rate of algorithmic progress by combining METR time-horizon data with Epoch AI compute estimates; median about 3.5x/year, 95% interval about 1.9-37x/year; the elasticity estimate is fitted only on two model families, Qwen-2.5 and Llama-3.1. [Same as above]
- 2024, RE-Bench (relayed by the podcast The Cognitive Revolution): on seven open-ended ML R&D tasks, 2024 models (Claude 3.5 Sonnet, o1) performed within 8 hours at about the 10th-40th percentile of professional ML researchers; original numbers not verified. [Source: cognitiverevolution.ai]
- How far the public evidence goes: METR has given a framework for splitting costs, but no public report directly gives a dollar or percentage split by "method progress vs pure compute trial and error". The existing numbers (a single run's $22,000, gains of only 1-1.5%, a progress-rate interval spanning about 20x) all point to self-improvement being small in scale and highly uncertain; the methodological contribution independent of compute has not been quantified.
