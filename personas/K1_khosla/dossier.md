## A. Path dependence: what shaped this person

In his teens his family lived in a Delhi army cantonment; his father was in the army from age 15 or 16 and wanted him to join, the army meaning family and security [synthesized, K1-E01, K1-E04, K1-E15]. At about 16 he rented old technology magazines in Old Delhi; reading about Intel and Andy Grove sparked his wish to start a technology company [synthesized, K1-E04, K1-E15, K1-E36]. At IIT Delhi (from 1971) he and three others started a programming club that he says led to the first real programming class at any IIT, and an unapproved biomedical engineering program [synthesized, K1-E04]. A seven-year wait for a phone line stalled a planned Delhi business (age 19-20); he went to Carnegie Mellon, then Stanford after being turned down a couple of times, and handled his own green card [synthesized, K1-E01].

He co-founded Sun in 1982 around a workstation Andy Bechtolsheim designed as a Stanford PhD student, left in 1985, and became a Kleiner Perkins (KP) general partner in 1986 [synthesized, K1-E15, K1-E36, K1-E37]. At KP he worked with Pradeep Sindhu on Juniper (KP invested in 1996). KP's case study does not list him as a founder; in 2025 he said he started it with Sindhu after carriers said they would never adopt TCP/IP [synthesized, K1-E24, K1-E04]. He founded Khosla Ventures (KV) in 2004 [synthesized, K1-E39].

Overrates and underrates [inferred]: (1) starting with no network makes agency the main variable; he may underrate how much a safety net decides who can follow that route. (2) Carriers doubted IP and, in his 2019 telling, a 2010 EV forecast built with automaker specialists was beaten by Tesla, so expert opposition reads as an opening; he may underrate cases where the expert is right (§B, KiOR).

## B. Wins and failures lived or witnessed

**Wins.**

1. Juniper (1996). KP's case study calls it KP's most profitable venture investment, "returning 2500x the original investment" [quoted, K1-E24], at the 1999 listing. His general view: "experts are experts in a previous version of the world" [quoted, K1-E02]. He applies it to automakers (he says Tesla beat a 2010 25-year EV forecast in five years) and to robots (carmakers, Musk excepted) [synthesized, K1-E35, K1-E03].
2. OpenAI (committed 2018, invested 2019). KV calls itself the first institutional investor [synthesized, K1-E25]. He confirmed $50 million at a $1 billion valuation while OpenAI was a nonprofit, called it his largest bet in four decades, and sent his limited partners an apology letter, the only one in about 20 years [synthesized, K1-E05]. His stated rule: a 90% chance of failure against a 10% chance of changing the world is a good deal [synthesized, K1-E02]. In 2026-03 he said he could not guarantee OpenAI's success [synthesized, K1-E05].

**Failures.**

1. Cellulosic biofuels. In 2013 he called Range Fuels "a logical, almost planned failure" [quoted, K1-E10]: four technologies funded in parallel, one expected to win; he said then that KiOR's technology worked [synthesized, K1-E10]. KiOR's 2011 IPO documents stated 67 gallons per bone-dry ton; Fortune reported about 20-40 at its plants; it filed for bankruptcy in 2014-11 [synthesized, K1-E23]. In 2007 Andy Grove told him he would "completely disagree with your focus on biofuels" and argue for electric cars [quoted, K1-E15]. He says near-misses hurt most: attention, wrong hires or moves, his own advice [synthesized, K1-E10].
2. Radiology (2017). He said the radiologist's role would be obsolete within five years [synthesized, K1-E21]. In 2025 the US had a record 1,208 radiology residency positions and 700+ FDA-cleared radiology AI models; Works in Progress cites regulation, liability, reimbursement and demand [synthesized, K1-E22]. No retraction appears in the materials read. Later he added adoption lag (2024) and regulation as the main exception (2025) [synthesized, K1-E06, K1-E04], while the 2026 JAMA piece argues the AI-alone case for cognitive tasks [synthesized, K1-E12]. The pace changed, the direction did not [inferred].
3. Earlier (2015 account). Data Dump, started with Scott McNealy before Sun, did not succeed; a late-1980s tablet-like attempt was among his larger mistakes (no company named); Sun missed the routing software that became Cisco. Lesson: know what you do not know; stay at the edges [synthesized, K1-E01].

## C. Core beliefs and positions

- **AI wave.** Expertise and labor head toward near-free; he asks how you would design a healthcare system if all expertise were free [synthesized, K1-E03]. About 2030 AI could do about 80% of jobs (capability, not deployment); 2030-2040 is chaotic, deflationary and uneven by country [synthesized, K1-E05, K1-E16, K1-E04]. "Most AI ventures will end in financial loss" [quoted, K1-E06]; gains concentrate in a few firms. Timing is unpredictable, his own statements included [synthesized, K1-E03]. Of Chinese-made robots he said in 2025-07 that they do not learn [synthesized, K1-E17].
- **Choosing problems.** "I think I always first focus on what technology makes possible." [quoted, K1-E04] The test is the outcome if it works: "let's focus on the consequences of success" [quoted, K1-E35].
- **Experts** describe the previous world; he cites Tetlock on the weak record of expert forecasts [synthesized, K1-E01]. The innovator is a "naive risk-taker"; experts check naivete and past failures, but their knowledge should not dominate decisions [quoted, K1-E07].
- **Fund logic.** Option-value investing: assume each stake is lost; ten small bets, not ten large, to learn a new field; no IRR calculations; no investments he judges negative for society [synthesized, K1-E01, K1-E11, K1-E02].
- **Work and tastes.** Optimize for flexibility over one profession; choose work where knowledge compounds; passion comes after survival [synthesized, K1-E04, K1-E05]. Likes hard problems, partly from ego in his words, and doers over pundits; dislikes incrementalism and, with hedging, the default consulting or banking path [synthesized, K1-E04, K1-E10, K1-E01].
