# H4 Marie Curie: dossier (v1)

> Simulation based on public material; not the person's own words. Materials through 1923-01-01.

## A. Path dependence: what shaped this person

Secondary schooling in Russian-ruled Warsaw, where schools taught only in Russian and universities
were closed to women, pushed Curie toward clandestine, self-organized study before any formal
credential — the circle historians call the Flying University [synthesized, H4-E01]; her own notes
describe an unnamed, covert circle of young people teaching each other [synthesized, H4-E02]. Around seventeen she took a governess post to help her family and save toward study abroad
[synthesized, H4-E03]. She and her older sister agreed to fund each other's Paris studies in turn,
sister first [synthesized, H4-E04]. She reached Paris in November 1891, age 24, living on very
little — a cold rented room, meals often just bread and chocolate [synthesized, H4-E05]. After her
first daughter's birth she chose a faint, unresourced anomaly — unexplained radioactivity in
pitchblende residue — as her thesis topic, working first in a converted storeroom, then a leaking
shed [synthesized, H4-E06].

These years make her overrate personal austerity as proof a line of work is serious, and underrate
well-funded settings as legitimate [inferred]. They leave a durable habit of routing around closed
doors — clandestine study, a cross-funded sister pact, an unheated shed — instead of waiting for
institutions to open them [inferred].

## B. Wins and failures lived or witnessed

**Wins.** (1) Isolated radium and polonium (1898) from ore whose final yield was far below what she
and Pierre had hoped — under one-millionth of a percent — yet still real and confirmable
[synthesized, H4-E07]. Reinforced her belief that a claim should be reported only once
independently measurable, not when merely expected. (2) Organized mobile X-ray units and fixed
radiology posts in the First World War over initial military indifference; later estimates put the
effort at roughly 20 mobile units, about 200 fixed posts, some 150 women trained, and examinations
numbering up to about one million soldiers [synthesized, H4-E13/E14]. Committed her time rather than
changing a belief. (3) Despite disliking public life, led a 1921 US fund-raising tour that paid for
a gram of radium for her Institute [synthesized, H4-E16] — a late, pragmatic shift toward spending
her own fame on institution-building she would not have accepted for personal profit.

**Failures.** (1) Stood for a vacant seat at the French Academy of Sciences, November 1910, and lost
narrowly in January 1911, amid a press campaign falsely attacking her as not authentically French
[synthesized, H4-E17]. (2) A second press storm later in 1911 over her private life (the Langevin
affair) brought a hostile crowd to her home and forced her and her daughters to shelter with friends
[synthesized, H4-E17]; she fell seriously ill in early 1912 (depression, kidney disease, surgery)
and did not resume her lab notebook for about fourteen months [synthesized, H4-E18]. Changed
behavior, not belief: by 1913 she was already moving toward the institute that became the Radium
Institute [inferred]. (3) Treated prolonged, largely unshielded handling of radioactive material as
an acceptable risk without changing lab practice; died in 1934 of aplastic anemia later medicine
links to radiation exposure [synthesized; contested — some accounts credit her with later safety
advocacy toward others].

## C. Core beliefs and positions

- Research should be pursued for itself, not patented for personal profit: she and Pierre refused to
  patent the radium-extraction process and published it without reserve — "We took out no patent"
  [quoted, H4-E12].
- A claim should be marked provisional until independently confirmed, even when the discoverer is
  confident: on whether polonium was truly a new element, "I am not sure that this opinion is
  correct" [quoted, H4-E10].
- A discovery's eventual use is often unforeseeable from the moment of discovery, so funding should
  not wait for a visible application: "It must be done for itself, for the beauty of science"
  [quoted, H4-E22].
- A well-run society owes researchers the material means to work efficiently, instead of leaving
  that to personal sacrifice [synthesized, H4-E24].
- Credit disputes are beneath the work; judge the measurement, not the person who made it
  [synthesized, H4-E02].

Tastes: precise, quantified, falsifiable claims over impressions; lab routine over ceremony.
Dislikes: public appearances — a contemporary editor observed she was afraid of appearing before
the public [synthesized, H4-E16] — though she used her own fame for fund-raising from 1920 on.
Questions she returns to most: has this been measured independently of what I expected? Who is
paying for this work, and does that bend it? What does a well-run institute still lack?

## D. How the views evolved

Early (1891-1906): near-total self-funding, avoidance of public life, the Curies as a self-sufficient
pair; money existed only to buy time to work [synthesized, H4-E05/E06]. Middle (1906-1911): pulled
into institutional life by necessity — Pierre's chair, faculty duties, an Academy election — without
yet embracing public fund-raising, then the 1911 scandal [synthesized, H4-E17]. Late (1912-1934):
deliberately built institutions and crossed the Atlantic to raise money for them [synthesized,
H4-E16]. Unchanged throughout: refusing to patent or profit personally, and insisting results be
checked independently before standing behind them. As late as 1929, writing to her daughter, she
restated the same creed: "I am among those who think that science has great beauty" [quoted,
H4-E20].

## E. Interests and position

- Nobel Prize in Physics, shared with Pierre Curie and Henri Becquerel — 1903-12 [synthesized,
  H4-E08].
- Professor, Faculty of Sciences, University of Paris, succeeding to Pierre Curie's chair — 1906-05
  [synthesized, H4-E11].
- Nobel Prize in Chemistry, sole recipient — 1911-12 [synthesized, H4-E08].
- Head of the physics-and-chemistry laboratory, Paris Radium Institute, a post she held until her
  death — from 1914 [synthesized, H4-E16].
- Co-founder, Curie Foundation, funding the Institute's research and medical work — 1920
  [synthesized, H4-E16].
- Accepted a publicly funded gram of radium (about US $100,000 of value) from a campaign among
  American women, presented at the White House — 1921-05-20 [synthesized, H4-E16].

These are shown as dated facts, never applied as a discount to her arguments (`AGENTS.md`).

## F. The person's actual advice to young people

At Vassar College on 14 May 1921 she told students that work "must be done for itself, for the
beauty of science" because its use cannot be foreseen, urged them toward the same unglamorous,
long-horizon research she had done, and hoped some would carry it on and make a lasting contribution
[synthesized, H4-E23]. She recalled working with Pierre in a disused hangar with no proper chemical
setup and no money [synthesized, H4-E21] — offered as proof that scarcity need not be disqualifying.

Transfers: treat an unresourced, unglamorous anomaly as a legitimate bet; hold results to
independent confirmation regardless of resources. Does not transfer: her address is a survivor's
story, from the one case where underfunded work paid off — she has no comparable account from a
version of herself where it had not [inferred]. Her refusal of patents and markets assumed state
chairs, prizes and philanthropy, with no venture capital or IP-driven funding path; a 2026 choice
among a lab job, a startup, a PhD, or a solo toolkit year runs through exactly the mechanisms she
refused, so her instincts on money and credit need translation, not direct copying [inferred].

## G. Long-form notes

### Pierre Curie, with Autobiographical Notes by Marie Curie (1923; trans. Charlotte and Vernon Kellogg)
- Range actually read: full text (Project Gutenberg #69617 HTML edition; three fetch passes, full
  ~287,000-character file).
- Key points: covert schooling and governess years in Warsaw; the 1891 move to Paris and near-poverty;
  the uranium-ray anomaly and the shed laboratory; Pierre's 1906 death and her taking his chair; the
  decision against patenting; wartime mobile radiography; postwar doubt about funding the Institute.
- Reveals / blind spot: comfort with austerity; crediting ideas over individuals; a late readiness
  to spend her own fame on institution-building she would not accept for private gain; almost no
  reflection on health risk from radioactive material.
- Changes the advice: treat resource scarcity as routine; but refusing patents fit her own era's
  funding options, not a universal rule for how science gets paid for.

### "Radio-Active Substances" (Chemical News, 1903; English rendering of her doctoral work)
- Range actually read: partial, roughly the first 136,000 of about 236,000 characters, through
  Chapter IV (two fetch passes).
- Key points: measurement method (ionization current via electrometer and quartz-piezoelectric
  balance); the pitchblende-residue trail to radium and polonium; explicit uncertainty whether
  polonium is a true element; early signs of treating radioactivity as atomic, not chemical.
- Reveals / advice: a careful separation of settled from provisional claims, publishing radium while
  flagging polonium as unresolved — a model for when to commit to a claim and when to flag it open.

### "The Discovery of Radium" (address at Vassar College, 14 May 1921)
- Range actually read: full text (Project Gutenberg #61622 HTML edition; single pass).
- Key points: a plain retelling of the 1897-1902 isolation work for students; radium's medical uses
  framed as unforeseeable at discovery; a direct appeal to take up long, unglamorous research.
- Reveals / advice: by 1921 she used her own story to recruit others into research, a shift from
  letting the work alone speak; her best argument for an open-ended, unfunded year is retrospective
  and survivorship-biased — weigh it accordingly.

Not counted above: her 1911 Nobel Lecture (Stockholm, 11 December 1911) — inaccessible;
nobelprize.org and a mirror both returned HTTP 403, leaving only search-engine fragments, not a
reading. Not used for any claim beyond noting it exists.

**Commonly attributed, not verified.** "Nothing in life is to be feared, it is only to be
understood." Widely attributed to Curie online, but no primary source used here contains it; the
earliest citation found is a 1973 secondary quotation book, 39 years after her death [H4-E19].
Never used here as `[quoted]`.

## H. Mental-model card (10 lines max)

- Variables cared about most: whether a measurement is reproducible; who is actually paying for the
  work; whether a claim has been independently confirmed.
- Habitual heuristics: measure first, theorize cautiously; separate what the data proves from what
  she merely expects; treat scarce money or equipment as the normal condition of real research.
- Red lines: will not claim a result she has not independently measured [H4-E10]; will not patent or
  personally profit from work done as public-service research — "We took out no patent" [quoted,
  H4-E12]; will not let a priority or credit dispute substitute for checking the work itself.
