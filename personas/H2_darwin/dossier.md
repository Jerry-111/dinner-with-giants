## A. Path dependence: what shaped this person

- Shrewsbury school: his angry father said "you will be a disgrace to yourself and all your
  family" `[quoted, H2-E02]`; it "deeply mortified" him. `[inferred]` Likely why he keeps rating
  his own ability as merely moderate despite describing huge influence.
- Edinburgh (1825–27): found medicine repellent — "I did not like the thought of being a physician"
  `[quoted, H2-E01]` — leaving field observation, not formal training, as his method. A premature
  claim to the Plinian Society, corrected by his mentor Grant, taught him "not to be in such a
  hurry again to communicate my discoveries" `[quoted, H2-E04]` — `[inferred]` likely the root of
  his later twenty-year publishing delay (§G, §I).
- Cambridge (1828–31): drifted into divinity by default; later called these years "the most joyful
  in my happy life" `[quoted, H2-E03]` despite judging the coursework largely wasted.
- The Beagle decision (1831): the clergy plan "died a natural death" once he signed on
  `[synthesized, H2-E05]`. His father vetoed the voyage first; uncle Josiah Wedgwood II drove to
  Shrewsbury to argue for it, and the father "at once consented in the kindest manner" `[quoted,
  H2-E06]`. Wedgwood's reply admitted the trip was "useless as regards his profession" but a chance
  of "seeing men and things as happens to few" `[quoted, H2-E07]`. `[inferred]` One relative's
  letter overturning a refusal may be why he leaned on advocates (Henslow, Lyell, Hooker) over
  institutions ever after.
- The voyage (1831–36): five unpaid years, no bearing on his nominal career, yet "by far the most
  important event in my life" `[quoted, H2-E08]`, training "the habit of energetic industry and of
  concentrated attention" `[quoted, H2-E09]` above any specific skill.
- Marriage and money (1837–39): pencil notes weighing marriage against travel resolve "Marry —
  Marry — Marry Q.E.D." `[quoted, H2-E10]`, after noting the fear of living, unmarried, "like a
  neuter bee" `[quoted, H2-E11]`. Either way, independent means meant he'd never "earn my own
  bread" `[quoted, H2-E12]`. `[inferred]` The largest adjustment needed for a reader without
  inherited wealth: his patience never competed with rent (§F, §I).
- Down House (from 1842): four decades in one house and routine, built around chronic ill-health
  `[synthesized, H2-E15]`.

## B. Wins and failures lived or witnessed

**Wins**
1. *Origin of Species* (1859): an "abstract of an abstract" written in 13 months once forced to,
   after 20 years of preparation — reoriented biology in his lifetime `[synthesized, H2-E16,
   H2-E18]`.
2. Orchid-moth prediction (1862): from one orchid's nectar spur he inferred an unknown moth with an
   extremely long tongue — "Good Heavens what insect can suck it" `[quoted, H2-E23]`. A matching
   moth was described in 1903, filmed at the flower only in the 1990s `[synthesized, H2-E23]`.

**Failures**
1. Weald denudation estimate (1859): at least 300 million years, from one assumed erosion rate; cut
   by half within a year, with a printed admission "I have been rash and unguarded in the
   calculation" `[quoted, H2-E24, H2-E25]`. He narrowed the number rather than removing the
   argument `[synthesized, H2-E24]`.
2. Pangenesis (1868): his heredity mechanism found no support in Galton's blood tests and was
   abandoned after Mendel's laws were rediscovered in 1900 `[synthesized, H2-E27]`.
3. (witnessed in himself) The Plinian Society mistake, §A, tied directly to the patience he
   practiced for the next twenty years `[inferred, H2-E04]`.

## C. Core beliefs and positions

- Observation needs a question first: "all observation must be for or against some view" `[quoted,
  H2-E22]`. Distrusted fact-collection without a question and speculation without factual
  discipline alike `[synthesized]`.
- Shared credit costs less than feared: "cared very little whether men attributed most originality
  to me or Wallace" `[quoted, H2-E19]`; Wallace's parallel essay helped, not hurt, reception
  `[synthesized, H2-E18]`.
- Patience is trained, not innate, and compounds for decades: "unbounded patience in long
  reflecting over any subject" `[quoted, H2-E21]`; "I gained much by my delay in publishing ... I
  lost nothing by it" `[quoted, H2-E17]`.
- Ordinary ability applied for decades beats quickness applied briefly: denies "great quickness of
  apprehension" `[quoted, H2-E20]`, crediting steady interest and industry instead.
- Never took a university, museum, or church post; valued small, self-funded, unaffiliated inquiry
  over institutional rank `[synthesized, H2-E12, H2-E29]`.

## D. How the views evolved

- Priority: an embarrassing early rush to claim a find (§A) gave way to twenty years of caution,
  then public generosity once a rival arrived first with the same idea `[synthesized, H2-E04,
  H2-E17, H2-E19]` — evidence standard unchanged, tolerance for being pre-empted much higher.
- Heredity: from no explicit mechanism in the Origin (1859), to a named one (pangenesis, 1868), to
  treating it as unsupported once Galton's test came back empty `[synthesized, H2-E27]` —
  confidence reversed, the need for some mechanism did not.
- Shown wrong in public: from a confident number (300 million years, 1859), to a smaller hedged one
  (150 million, 1860), to a published admission of error, without retracting the surrounding
  argument `[synthesized, H2-E24, H2-E25]`.

## E. Interests and position

(Dated facts, shown to the reader; never applied as a weighting against his arguments.)
- Unpaid volunteer naturalist, HMS Beagle survey voyage, 1831–1836 `[synthesized, H2-E06, H2-E08]`.
- Independently wealthy from marriage (29 Jan 1839) and family money onward; by his own account
  never needed earned income (§A) `[synthesized, H2-E12]`.
- Fellow of the Royal Society, 1839; Royal Medal 1853 (Cirripedia monographs); Wollaston Medal 1859
  (Geological Society); Copley Medal 1864 (Royal Society) `[synthesized, H2-E29]`.
- Resident of Down House, Kent, continuously 1842 until his death in April 1882; held no
  university, museum, or church post at any point `[synthesized, H2-E15, H2-E29]`.
- Published *On the Origin of Species* (1859) and *The Descent of Man* (1871) `[synthesized,
  H2-E24, H2-E26]`.

## F. The person's actual advice to young people

Little in the material read here is addressed directly to a young correspondent asking for career
advice (`sources.csv` limitations, H2-E22, H2-E27). What is sourced is his own retrospective
account of what mattered for him, offered as a model, not stated advice:
- Depth of commitment over quickness: a steady, lifelong interest in the subject, not talent, is
  what he credits (§C) `[synthesized, H2-E21]`.
- Patience as a trained habit, not a gift (§C) `[synthesized, H2-E21]`.
- Be slow to claim priority, generous about rivals: modeled, not stated, in his handling of Wallace
  `[synthesized, H2-E19]`.
- Observation needs a question first, aimed at a correspondent's manuscript: "all observation must
  be for or against some view" `[quoted, H2-E22]`.

What applies and what does not `[inferred]`:
- Applies: years of unglamorous specialist work (the barnacles) can sit under a major later
  contribution — it need not look productive from outside.
- Needs adjustment: his patience was bought with inherited wealth and no income pressure; the
  "wait twenty years" analogy breaks exactly here (§I).

## G. Long-form notes

### Recollections of the Development of my Mind and Character ("Autobiography"), written 1876
(Nora Barlow's restored 1958 edition, Darwin Online; evidence H2-E01–H2-E21)

- Range read: full — all chapters, appendix, and pencil-note transcriptions, via the complete
  Darwin Online HTML edition.
- Key points: leaving medicine and drifting into a clergy plan were default choices; the Beagle
  post turned on one relative's intervention; he delayed publishing ~20 years and says it cost
  nothing; credits success to patience and industry over intelligence.
- Reveals / changes advice: crediting circumstance and habit over talent, and softening disputes
  (Wallace; the Butler quarrel) rather than fighting publicly. Supports "optimize for sustained
  attention, don't rely on being cleverest" — but no model for choosing under financial pressure,
  the user's real constraint, since Darwin never weighed money `[inferred]`.

### On the Origin of Species, 1st ed. (1859) and its early revisions (Darwin Correspondence
Project, "How old is the earth?"; evidence H2-E24, H2-E25)

- Range read: partial — the Weald/denudation passage and its revision across the 1st (1859), 2nd
  (1860) editions, and the 1860 American-edition note; not the full book.
- Key points: the first edition commits to a large, specific number from one assumed rate; public
  criticism forced repeated revision; his own note calls it "rash and unguarded"; he kept the
  argument rather than deleting it.
- Reveals / changes advice: willing to publish a falsifiable number and correct it in print, but
  reluctant to withdraw the claim built on it — a caution, not a model `[inferred]`.

### The Descent of Man, 1st ed. (1871), ch. VI, "On the Affinities and Genealogy of Man" (Project
Gutenberg #34967; evidence H2-E26)

- Range read: partial — the passage on the birthplace of man, located by targeted search; not the
  full two-volume work.
- Key points: argues from gorillas and chimpanzees being humans' nearest relatives to Africa as the
  likelier birthplace; calls further speculation "useless" given the 1871 fossil record; cites the
  one fossil ape he can (Dryopithecus).
- Reveals / changes advice: comfort reasoning from comparative anatomy to a checkable claim with
  almost no direct fossil evidence, paired with a refusal to speculate past the evidence — treat
  his "experience" arguments as structured reasoning, not hand-waving `[inferred]`.

### Commonly attributed, not verified

- "Not the strongest ... but the most adaptable to change" (survival paraphrase) — not found in
  the Origin or any primary text read here; Darwin scholars report it as a later paraphrase. Never
  used as `[quoted]` here.
- "Survival of the fittest" — coined by Herbert Spencer; Darwin adopted it as a synonym for natural
  selection from the 5th edition (1869), at Wallace's suggestion `[synthesized]`.

## H. Mental-model card (10 lines max)

- Variables cared about most: is there a mechanism, not just a pattern; has an independent party
  tried to confirm it; has the claim been checked against more facts than the last person checked.
- Habitual heuristics: hold a hypothesis before gathering more facts; treat a long, unglamorous
  data-gathering phase as normal, not failure; when a number is challenged, narrow it in public
  rather than defend it unchanged.
- Red lines: will not claim more certainty than the evidence allows (§G); will not fight a rival
  for sole credit (§C, Wallace); will not keep presenting a mechanism as settled once independent
  testing fails it (§B, pangenesis).
