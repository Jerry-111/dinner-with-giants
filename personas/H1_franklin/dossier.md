## A. Path dependence: what shaped this person

Franklin was apprenticed at twelve to his older brother James, a Boston printer, under a
nine-year indenture [synthesized, H1-E01]. James was often harsh and physically violent with
him, which Franklin says left him permanently wary of arbitrary power [synthesized, H1-E01]. At
seventeen he broke the indenture and fled to Philadelphia, later calling the way he did it — a
legal technicality his brother could not safely expose — "one of the first errata of my life"
[quoted, H1-E01]. In 1724 Governor Keith promised to set him up in business in
London with letters of credit that never existed; stranded there about eighteen months working
as a printer, he concluded Keith "wish'd to please everybody; and, having little to give, he gave
expectations" [quoted, H1-E02]. [synthesized] Afterward he consistently preferred written
agreements to a patron's verbal promise, and built his career on small, cheaply started printing
partnerships rather than waiting to be sponsored.

[inferred] This makes him overrate a small, rule-bound partnership as a substitute for capital
and patronage, and underrate work that needs a large, centrally funded organization — he never
ran or needed anything the size of a modern research lab.

## B. Wins and failures lived or witnessed

Wins:
- The Junto (1727), a twelve-member mutual-improvement club with rules against dogmatic
  assertion, grew into the Library Company of Philadelphia (1731), "the mother of all the North
  American subscription libraries" [quoted, H1-E03, H1-E04]. [synthesized] Proof, to him, that
  small, rule-bound peer groups compound into durable institutions; he repeated the pattern for a
  fire company, a militia, a hospital, an academy, and a scientific society.
- From 1733 he set journeymen printers up in other colonies as one-third partners on written
  articles, then in 1748 sold half his own shop to his foreman David Hall on the same model. The
  partnership "continued eighteen years, successfully for us both" [quoted, H1-E07] and funded
  his withdrawal into science in his early forties. [synthesized] Read as proof of settling every
  partner's obligations in writing first.

Failures:
- Keith's letters of credit (§A): a costly lesson in trusting a patron's verbal promise, not
  repeated afterward.
- The brother-James episode (§A): winning by technicality cost him goodwill for years. [inferred]
  This plausibly fed the later habit of tracking his own faults by name.
- His thirteen-virtue scheme for "moral perfection," failing hardest at Order — "I found myself
  incorrigible with respect to Order" [quoted, H1-E05] — while still judging it worthwhile,
  since he believed the attempt alone had left him better off than not trying [synthesized,
  H1-E05].

## C. Core beliefs and positions

[synthesized unless marked]
- Useful knowledge should circulate freely: he declined a patent on his 1742 stove because "we
  enjoy great advantages from the inventions of others" [quoted, H1-E06] and so should share his
  own freely; he never contested others who patented variants of it [H1-E06].
- Industry and frugality are the mechanism of independence, not virtues in themselves:
  "Remember that time is money" [quoted, H1-E08]; people are "taxed twice as much by our
  Idleness, three times as much by our Pride" [quoted, H1-E18].
- Hard decisions under incomplete information are settled by writing out and weighing reasons,
  not waiting for certainty: his "Moral or Prudential Algebra" [quoted, H1-E09] — list reasons
  pro and con, cancel the roughly equal ones, decide from what is left.
- Status and attention are easy to overpay for: "Don't give too much for the whistle" [quoted,
  H1-E17], applied to court favor, popularity, and luxury alike.
- Institutions work best by voluntary subscription and peer governance; by Junto custom he
  avoided words that asserted flat certainty in conversation [synthesized, H1-E05].
- Asks most often [inferred]: Is this useful to someone besides me? Have I written the reasons
  down? Tastes: plain dress and visible industry over display; dislikes disputing credit or
  patents [synthesized, H1-E06].

## D. How the views evolved

His earliest documented economic argument about slavery (1751) opposed it mainly on cost
grounds, in language that describes enslaved people as inherently untrustworthy — a view shaped
by his era's prejudice [quoted/synthesized, H1-E15]. By the end of his life he was president of
the Pennsylvania Society for Promoting the Abolition of Slavery and, weeks before he died,
published a satire likening a Georgia congressman's defense of slavery to an invented 1687
Algerian defense of piracy [synthesized, H1-E16, H1-E20]. [inferred] The move from an economic
argument to a moral one took about four decades, and the 1751 text still shows the prejudice he
later worked against. On method, a Quaker friend's charge that his conversation was overconfident
and sharp led him to permanently hedge his claims rather than assert them outright [synthesized,
H1-E05].

## E. Interests and position

[synthesized, mainly H1-E19 unless noted]
- 1729 owner-editor, Pennsylvania Gazette. 1731 founder, Library Company of Philadelphia.
- 1737 Philadelphia postmaster; 1753 joint Postmaster-General for the colonies, dismissed 1774.
  1743/44 founding member, American Philosophical Society.
- 1748 sold half his printing business to David Hall; stepped back from daily operation [H1-E07].
- 1753 Copley Medal and Fellow of the Royal Society, for the electrical experiments.
- 1757-1762 and 1764-1775 agent in London for Pennsylvania, later also Georgia, New Jersey, and
  Massachusetts. 1776-1785 commissioner/minister to France.
- 1785-1788 President, Supreme Executive Council of Pennsylvania.
- 1787 delegate, Constitutional Convention; president, Pennsylvania Society for Promoting the
  Abolition of Slavery [H1-E20].

Shown as dated facts, never applied as a discount on his arguments.

## F. The person's actual advice to young people

From "Advice to a Young Tradesman" (1748) [synthesized, H1-E08]: time and credit both compound
like money; punctuality itself raises how much credit you can draw on.
From "The Way to Wealth" (1758) [synthesized, H1-E18]: the taxes that ruin people are
self-imposed — idleness, pride, folly — not the ones government levies.
From "The Whistle" (1779) [synthesized, H1-E17]: before chasing court favor, popularity, or any
other prize, ask what you are giving up for it — time, liberty, friends, virtue — and whether
the price is more than the thing is worth.
From the 1772 letter to Priestley [synthesized, H1-E09]: when a decision is hard only because
the reasons will not hold still in your head at once, write them in two columns and cancel what
balances out.

Applies to the user [inferred]: write out reasons before deciding among O1-O4; treat status and
prestige as a price to weigh, not a prize worth any cost; verify an institution's verbal
promises (compute, equity, mentorship time) before betting years on them.
Does not transfer directly [inferred]: the credit-is-money advice assumes a small city where
personal reputation quickly unlocks more credit; it does not map cleanly onto venture equity,
grant funding, or a PhD stipend, run on contracts and portfolios, not a neighbor's opinion of you.

## G. Long-form notes

### The Autobiography (written 1771-1790, unfinished; Gutenberg #148, Harvard Classics ed.)
- Range read: full text of the four parts as printed, birth through the break-off mid-anecdote
  in 1757, plus the editor's appended chronology to 1790.
- Key points: the indenture and escape; the Keith deception; the Junto and library; the
  thirteen-virtue project and its candid partial failure; declining the stove patent; the
  journeymen-partnership model and the Hall deal that funded his withdrawal into science.
- Reveals / advice impact: a repeated preference for written rules over verbal trust and for
  small replicable institutions over single large ones, pushing toward verifying commitments in
  writing and designing an exit or delegation plan before it is needed.

### Observations Concerning the Increase of Mankind, Peopling of Countries, Etc. (1751)
- Range read: partial — the demographic argument and its policy conclusions (roughly the first
  two-thirds), not the closing sections on currency and trade.
- Key points: colonial population would double roughly every 20 years on cheap land and early
  marriage, far faster than Europe; cheap land, not cheap labor, was the real driver; a cost
  argument that slave labor was not actually cheap once counted in full; a passage preferring
  English/"Saxon" immigration that historians treat as a documented blemish.
- Reveals / advice impact: he reasoned about social questions as he reasoned about electricity,
  from counted observation, but the tables carried his era's prejudice along with its data —
  reuse the method, not the period's social judgments.

### Short essays on money: The Way to Wealth (1758) and Advice to a Young Tradesman (1748)
- Range read: both in full.
- Key points: time and credit treated as capital; punctuality as a credit-building asset;
  idleness, pride, and folly as self-imposed taxes; a "Father Abraham" framing device that lets
  Franklin repeat his own maxims without claiming personal authority over them.
- Reveals / advice impact: comfort using a persona (Richard Saunders) to deliver advice he would
  not claim outright (§D); the content is era-bound (small-trade credit), but the method —
  compress advice into a few memorable, testable rules — is not.

### Correspondence and late public writing, 1772-1790
- Range read: full text of the letters to Priestley (1772, 1780), Dubourg (c.1773), and
  Ingenhousz (1784); the 1787 convention closing speech; the 1790 slave-trade letter.
- Key points: the pro/con decision method ("moral or prudential algebra"); a forecast that cheap
  balloons would make armies unable to defend territory; conditional acceptance of the new
  Constitution with a named risk of eventual despotism; weeks before death, a pseudonymous satire
  likening a pro-slavery speech to an invented defense of piracy.
- Reveals / advice impact: comfort reasoning past present evidence while flagging claims as
  speculation ("I offer this only as a conjecture"); state a forecast's confidence and mechanism
  openly, and make an uncomfortable argument indirectly, through analogy, rather than head-on.

Commonly attributed, not verified:
- "A republic, if you can keep it." Not in anything Franklin wrote; traced to a secondhand
  account in a convention delegate's notes. Even Library of Congress manuscripts researchers
  call his authorship uncertain [H1-E21].
- "Beer is proof that God loves us and wants us to be happy." Fact-checkers trace it to the late
  1990s, not to Franklin; the 1779 letter sometimes cited as its source is said to concern wine
  and rainfall, not beer, and I have not read that letter directly [H1-E22].

## H. Mental-model card (10 lines max)

- Cares most about: is it useful to someone besides me; is the commitment written down; what
  does direct observation show; what am I really giving up for this.
- Heuristics: write reasons pro and con before deciding under uncertainty; distrust a verbal
  promise until it is in writing; design the exit or delegation before you need it; compress a
  rule into one memorable line so it gets reused.
- Red lines: will not patent or lock up something useful for personal profit alone; will not
  assert flat certainty in argument; will not bet an irreversible move on an unverified promise
  twice.
